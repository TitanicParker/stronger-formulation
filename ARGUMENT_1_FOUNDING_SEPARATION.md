# Argument 1 — The Founding Separation

**Status:** LOAD-BEARING DOCUMENT-ONLY CASE  
**Depends on the Protest:** No  
**Controlling source:** `SRC_COMPLETE_RECORD_FORENSIC.md`

---

# 1. The proposition

> **The patient entered Neurology with disabling painful feet inside an integrated feet–gait–tone–movement problem. Neurology correctly identified severe young-onset Parkinsonism and a dystonic-looking lower-limb phenomenon, but the surviving record does not clearly show what work established that the painful feet could safely be treated as separate from the neurological motor state.**

This is the case if every word of the Protest is rejected.

It does not depend on memory.

It does not depend on motive.

It does not depend on later genetics.

It begins with what the documents themselves show.

---

# 2. What entered Neurology

Primary care had already assembled the problem as one clinical picture:

- bilateral painful feet;
- abnormal feet;
- abnormal gait;
- marked stiffness and increased tone;
- difficulty initiating movement;
- toe clawing;
- declining function.

Podiatry was already appropriate because the structural foot disease was real.

But the GP still sent the patient to Neurology because the problem had not been reduced to structure alone.

The hospital therefore received an integrated question:

> **Why is this man in disabling pain when he uses his feet, and what does the neurological disorder have to do with it?**

---

# 3. What Neurology found

Neurology correctly recognised a profound neurological disorder.

The record contains:

- severe young-onset Parkinsonism;
- marked rigidity and slowness;
- a strikingly abnormal gait;
- a foot-drop-like lower-limb appearance described as suggestive of dystonic posture and not true foot drop;
- pes planus;
- hammertoes;
- longstanding corns and calluses;
- structural abnormalities of the feet.

So the case is not:

> nobody looked.

And it is not:

> nobody recognised the neurological disease.

The case is narrower and stronger:

> **They saw the abnormal neurological lower limb. They knew the feet hurt. What work showed what one had to do with the other?**

---

# 4. The missing bridge

The record contains a large amount of neurological detail and a large amount of foot detail.

What is difficult to identify is the bridge.

The surviving record does not clearly show how clinicians tested:

- the exact pain distribution;
- the dynamic posture of toes and ankles during standing and walking;
- whether deformity was fixed or movement-dependent;
- whether the abnormal gait loaded the painful areas;
- whether dystonic posture altered weight-bearing;
- whether there was positive sensory evidence for neuropathy;
- whether the structural lesions were primary, secondary, amplified or unrelated to the motor disorder.

Do not convert documentary absence into historical certainty.

The correct proposition is:

> **The record does not clearly demonstrate enough relationship-directed work to explain how the competing possibilities were tested before separation was allowed to become a live explanation.**

---

# 5. The first transformation

The specialist synthesis eventually says the foot/leg symptoms:

> **“may have been completely separate”**

and raises peripheral neuropathy before concluding:

> **“Not clear.”**

It also records that the patient had “markedly divorced” the foot pain from the bodily tension in his own mind.

That is an important patient observation.

But phenomenological distinction is not mechanistic independence.

The patient could genuinely experience the foot pain as different from bodily tension and the neurological state could still be contributing to posture, gait, loading, toe position, pressure, pain amplification or treatment response.

The question is therefore:

> **What clinical work earned the move from unresolved disabling pain to specialist uncertainty about a possibly separate foot problem?**

---

# 6. Podiatry does not answer the neurological relationship

Podiatry was appropriate.

The structural feet required treatment.

That point should be conceded fully.

But the existence of a Podiatry destination did not itself answer:

> **Why were these painful structures painful in this particular neurological patient?**

The distinction is:

> **treating the painful structures**

is not the same thing as:

> **determining the relationship between neurological motor state, gait, loading, structure and pain.**

The practical question becomes:

> **Who owned the whole problem once the feet and Neurology were being managed in different places?**

---

# 7. The medication evidence reopens the relationship

The original pain predates neurological treatment.

So the case is not that Neurology created the pain.

The important later fact is different:

> **Neurological medication changed what happened in the feet.**

During procyclidine withdrawal the patient reported tension building, restless feet and his walk falling apart.

The treating neurologist later recorded a focal outside-left-foot/corn phenomenon as among the first things changing or returning rapidly during withdrawal.

That does not prove the corn literally disappeared and reappeared.

It does show that a focal painful foot phenomenon changed with neurological treatment state.

The question becomes:

> **If a treatment prescribed for the neurological condition changed the foot problem, when did the original foot–neurology question have to be rebuilt?**

This is one of the strongest later routes because it does not depend on the Protest.

---

# 8. March 2019 and the disappearance of the destination

By March 2019 the GP again linked:

- feet;
- increased tone;
- abnormal gait;
- Parkinson’s disease;
- clawing and pain.

The Podiatry record identifies 25 March 2019 as the last recorded attendance.

After that date the practical destination disappears.

But the explanatory separation may survive.

The clean formulation is:

> **After March 2019, the feet are no longer elsewhere; the separation is.**

From then on, the question is less about allocation between two active services and more about inherited conceptual framing.

---

# 9. Later Neurology repeatedly crosses the old boundary

The later record includes:

- Parkinsonian-looking foot movement;
- normal foot/ankle power despite a foot-drop appearance;
- excessive foot movement and sensitivity;
- morning foot difficulty;
- severe bilateral foot/lower-leg pain;
- possible OFF dystonia;
- a Movement Disorders view that leg pain and shuffling appeared OFF-related and that the patient appeared undertreated;
- toe curling;
- pregabalin benefit;
- Sinemet-related relief from a pain perspective.

There is contrary evidence too.

At one stage the patient believed the pain was not Parkinson-related and reported no perceived Parkinson difference after stopping Parkinson medication for ten days.

The record is mixed.

That is precisely why the right question is not:

> was it all neurological?

It is:

> **When did the accumulated crossing evidence become strong enough that the original feet–gait–pain relationship had to be reconstructed from the beginning?**

---

# 10. The 2023 crisis strengthens the no-Protest case

The documentary case does not end in 2019 or 2020.

By 2023 the same pain problem has become severe enough to generate direct life-tolerability language and clinician-authored safety concern.

That means the no-Protest case is no longer merely:

> **Was the 2017 separation inadequately investigated?**

It is also:

> **What did reasonable care require once the unresolved pain became dangerous and the old explanatory architecture was still in play?**

Argument 3 addresses that second fork directly.

---

# 11. The strongest legal/expert formulation

> **Reasonable Neurology had to investigate whether the neurological disorder was contributing to the disabling foot problem before treating the feet as separate, or keep that question actively open if it could not yet be answered.**

Potential date-specific breach questions include:

1. whether enough relationship-directed assessment occurred in 2017;
2. whether “possibly separate” and neuropathy were supported by enough evidence;
3. whether Neurology retained ownership of the neurological part of the foot problem while Podiatry treated structure;
4. whether medication-linked foot changes required reopening;
5. whether later foot/gait/treatment evidence required global reconstruction rather than local explanation.

Every one of those remains expert-dependent.

---

# 12. Defence that must remain inside the argument

The defence is real and should be preserved:

- severe Parkinsonism was correctly diagnosed;
- structural foot disease was genuine;
- Podiatry was appropriate;
- the patient distinguished foot pain from bodily tension;
- later evidence need not retrospectively reconstruct 2017;
- later clinicians did reconsider dystonia, neuropathy and OFF phenomena;
- mixed mechanisms remain possible.

The response is not to deny those facts.

It is:

> **The issue is not whether structural disease existed or whether later reassessment occurred. The issue is what work justified separation at the founding episode and when later evidence required reconstruction.**

---

# 13. The one-line argument

> **They saw the abnormal neurological walk. They knew the feet hurt. The record does not clearly show what work earned the separation.**
