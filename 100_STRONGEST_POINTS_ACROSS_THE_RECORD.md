# The 100 Strongest Points Across the Record

**Status:** INTERNAL ANALYTICAL TREASURY

**Rule:** Plain facts first. Technical explanation later.

---

## 1–10: The centre

1. The patient had disabling foot pain before Neurology.
2. The source of that pain was unresolved.
3. The GP sent the feet, gait, tone and movement problem into Neurology together.
4. Neurology found a very abnormal neurological gait.
5. The lower limb looked dystonic rather than weak.
6. The feet were structurally abnormal as well.
7. The record does not clearly show the examination that explained how those things related.
8. The specialist account nevertheless said the foot problem **“may have been completely separate.”**
9. It also said **“Not clear.”**
10. The centre is: **unresolved source of disabling pain becomes specialist uncertainty about a possibly separate foot problem.**

## 11–20: What Neurology actually did

11. Neurology did not miss the Parkinsonism.
12. Neurology did not miss the abnormal gait.
13. Neurology did not miss the lower-limb abnormality.
14. The neurological examination was substantial.
15. The examination was videoed.
16. Physiotherapy and Occupational Therapy were involved.
17. The case is therefore not “nobody looked.”
18. The case is “what did the abnormal neurological gait have to do with the painful feet?”
19. Recognition of the sign is not the same as answering that relationship.
20. A detailed neurological diagnosis does not automatically answer a separate pain question.

## 21–30: The missing bridge

21. The record contains many facts about the feet.
22. The record contains many facts about the movement disorder.
23. What is hard to find is the bridge between them.
24. Where exactly did the feet hurt?
25. What happened to the toes and ankles while standing and walking?
26. Was the deformity fixed or did it change with movement?
27. Were the painful areas being loaded by the abnormal gait?
28. What formal sensory evidence supported neuropathy?
29. What examination showed the feet could safely be treated as separate?
30. Before asking whether “Not clear” was reasonable, ask whether the examination was capable of earning that answer.

## 31–40: The first separation

31. Structural foot disease was real.
32. Structural disease does not prove neurological independence.
33. Podiatry was appropriate.
34. Podiatry existed before the final Neurology synthesis.
35. The GP even told Podiatry the case was being prioritised **“as Parkinson's.”**
36. The problem is not that Podiatry was involved.
37. The problem is whether Podiatry treatment answered the neurological relationship.
38. Someone still had to ask what the abnormal gait was doing to the feet.
39. Someone still had to put Neurology and Podiatry findings together.
40. **Who was supposed to put the pieces back together?**

## 41–50: The patient's own warning

41. The patient said in substance: **“You have not dealt with my feet.”**
42. That did not create the problem.
43. It made the unfinished problem explicit.
44. The patient did not need to know the mechanism.
45. He only needed to know that the problem he came in with had not been answered.
46. The final portable specialist account did not preserve that protest.
47. Downstream clinicians therefore received a cleaner-looking closure than the bedside experience may have warranted.
48. “Feet unclear; Podiatry arranged” is different from “patient says the feet were not dealt with.”
49. That difference matters for future care.
50. The Protest turns a possible omission into express notice.

## 51–60: The tablets bring the case to life

51. The foot pain existed before neurological medication.
52. Neurology did not create the original pain.
53. Neurological medication nevertheless changed what happened in the feet.
54. Procyclidine produced major overall improvement.
55. When it was stopped, tension built up.
56. The feet became restless.
57. The walk fell apart.
58. The neurologist later documented a focal foot problem as returning rapidly during withdrawal.
59. That does not prove the pain was dystonia.
60. It does prove the foot problem was not therapeutically separate from Neurology.

## 61–70: What the medication response means

61. A treatment response does not have to prove one mechanism to matter.
62. It only has to make the old separation harder to leave unquestioned.
63. The proper question is not “did procyclidine prove dystonia?”
64. The proper question is “why did stopping neurological medication make the foot problem come back?”
65. That was new information after July.
66. New information creates a new duty to think again if it materially challenges the old explanation.
67. The April 2018 note is a later record of the earlier withdrawal event, not a second experiment.
68. By April 2018 the treating neurologist had personally documented the treatment-linked foot change.
69. Even if July had been reasonable, that later event could require reopening.
70. **The pain may have remained diagnostically separate, but it was not therapeutically separate.**

## 71–80: The later years

71. In 2019 primary care again linked feet, tone, gait and Parkinson's disease.
72. The last recorded Podiatry attendance was 25 March 2019.
73. After that, later active Podiatry treatment is not shown in the available record.
74. Neurology itself later recorded foot movement and sensitivity.
75. Neurology recorded morning foot problems.
76. Neurology later considered OFF dystonia.
77. A later Movement Disorders contact said leg pain and shuffling appeared OFF-related and that the patient appeared undertreated.
78. The patient also gave contrary evidence and at one stage thought the pain was not Parkinson-related.
79. The later record is therefore mixed, not simple.
80. The key point is that feet, walking and neurological treatment repeatedly interacted.

## 81–90: Harm and the institutional case

81. The pain remained a major problem for years.
82. By 2023 it was associated with major functional loss and serious distress.
83. The causal question is not whether Neurology created the original pain.
84. The question is whether delayed understanding or treatment made the existing pain worse or last longer.
85. Prolonged severe pain can itself contribute to psychological distress.
86. That causal chain needs expert proof.
87. Different services held different pieces of the problem.
88. The institutional case is strongest when tied to who actually knew what.
89. Do not say “the hospital knew everything.”
90. Ask instead: **who knew enough to ask the next obvious question?**

## 91–100: Legal structure in plain language

91. Duty: what should reasonable Neurology have done?
92. Breach: what, if anything, was not done that should have been?
93. Harm: what extra suffering followed?
94. Causation: what would better care probably have changed?
95. The case does not require proving all foot pain was dystonia.
96. The case does not require proving Podiatry was wrong.
97. The case does not require proving the Parkinsonism diagnosis was wrong.
98. The case does require asking what clinical work justified treating the painful feet as separate.
99. Later treatment response requires asking why the original question was not reopened.
100. The final question is: **if they had done the right thing at the right time, what suffering would probably have been avoided?**
