# 1. The centre of the case

> **Unresolved source of disabling pain**  
> **becomes**  
> **specialist uncertainty about a possibly separate foot problem.**

That remains the centre.

But the later record adds a second transformation:

> **The unresolved pain becomes a life-safety problem while the explanatory model governing that pain is itself expressly recognised as misaligned.**

The case is therefore not three unrelated allegations.

It is **one longitudinal case with three separable propositions and two major forks**:

1. **The Founding Separation** — survives without the Protest.
2. **The Protest Fork** — what the founding case becomes if the Protest is established.
3. **The Second Fork: Safety and Misalignment** — independently documentary from 2023 onward and does not depend on the Protest.

The master question is:

> **What happened each time the patient, the GP, treatment response or the clinical record itself showed that the existing explanation was no longer enough for the problem the patient was actually living with?**

---

# 2. The drafting rule

Use this order:

> **Quote first.  
> Fact second.  
> Question third.  
> Medicine fourth.  
> Law last.**

If something can be said accurately in ordinary language, say it that way first.

Do not lead with technical words when the fact is simpler.

Examples:

- not **“pharmacological dechallenge”** — say **“when they stopped the tablets, the foot problem came back quickly.”**
- not **“mechanistic independence”** — say **“were the feet really separate?”**
- not **“clinical segregation”** — say **“the foot problem was treated as separate.”**
- not **“treatment-state-sensitive pain phenotype”** — say **“the pain changed with neurological medication.”**
- not **“institutional information architecture”** — say **“who knew what, and who was supposed to put it together?”**
- not **“relationship-directed examination”** — say **“an examination to find out whether the neurological problem was affecting the feet.”**
- not **“reopening threshold”** — say **“this was a reason to look again.”**

Technical language stays where the medical or legal term itself matters. Otherwise ordinary language comes first.

# 3. Argument One — the Founding Separation

This is the strongest case **without** the Protest.

The patient did not arrive with two settled problems.

He arrived with disabling foot pain, abnormal feet, abnormal gait, increased tone/stiffness, movement-initiation difficulty, toe clawing and declining function.

Primary care had already involved Podiatry because there was real structural foot disease.

That did not answer the neurological question.

Neurology then found severe young-onset Parkinsonism, marked rigidity and slowness, a strikingly abnormal gait and a foot-drop-like appearance described as suggestive of dystonic posture and **not true foot drop**, together with pes planus and hammertoes.

The examination was substantial. The gait was videoed. Physiotherapy and Occupational Therapy were involved.

So the case is not:

> nobody looked;

or:

> nobody recognised the neurological problem.

The case is:

> **They saw the abnormal neurological lower limb. They knew the feet hurt. Where is the work showing why the two were separate?**

The surviving record contains many neurological observations and many foot observations.

What remains hard to find is the bridge between them: pain mapping, dynamic toe/ankle behaviour, loading, relationship to gait and positive examination support for neuropathy.

Do not say the missing examinations definitely never happened.

Say:

> **The record does not show enough work to explain how the competing possibilities were tested.**

Then the specialist synthesis says the foot symptoms **“may have been completely separate”**, raises possible peripheral neuropathy, and concludes **“Not clear.”** It also records that the patient had **“markedly divorced”** the foot pain from bodily tension in his own mind.

The controlling question is:

> **What work earned the separation?**

Structural pathology was real.

But:

> **Structural pathology is not structural independence.**

# 4. Argument Two — the Protest and the Disclosed Choice

This argument is **conditional on establishing the Protest as evidence**.

The patient says that before discharge he said, in substance:

> **You have not dealt with my feet.**

The exact phrase is not found verbatim in the contemporaneous canonical record. Later patient-authored material records that disabling bilateral foot pain was raised as unaddressed and that the supervising neurologist was requested. Keep that source distinction visible.

The Protest does not create the unfinished problem.

It does something more precise:

> **It turns possible omission into express notice while the original phenotype is still physically available for correction.**

Before the Protest, the question is whether the service appreciated that the source of disabling pain remained unresolved.

After the Protest, the question becomes:

> **What did the service choose to do once the patient had pointed directly at the unfinished problem?**

The possible futures were not binary. They included reopening the examination, a limited targeted return, senior or movement-disorders review, a defined Neurology–Podiatry question, explicit active uncertainty, reassurance, planned review, therapeutic observation, legitimate separation after directed assessment, documentation of disagreement, retained neurological ownership, or at minimum preservation of the Protest in the portable record.

The point is not that one particular response was automatically mandatory.

The point is:

> **Many clinically, humanly and documentarily responsible futures were still available.**

The Protest is therefore:

> **the widest point in the future.**

Everything after it is loss of optionality.

If the Protest occurred and did not travel in the portable specialist account, the omission does not merely remove a quotation.

> **It removes the fork from history.**

That is the legal spotlight: information available, choices available, course followed, professional standard, consequence.

# 5. Argument Three — the Second Fork: Pain, Safety and Misalignment

This argument does **not** depend on the Protest.

By 2023 the unresolved pain problem has changed status.

It is no longer only a source-of-pain question.

It has become a safety question.

## 1 February 2023 — direct patient notice

The patient emails Thomas Monaghan, Patrick Browne and Aaron Brennan.

He says the foot pain was **“The hardest part of the entire experience.”** He says the residual pain is **“absolutely intolerable though not unbearable.”** Then:

> **“I have no idea how I tolerated the pain, because I don’t think I could live with it anymore. It is perfect torture.”**

That is direct notice into the treating network.

It does not by itself prove suicidal intent.

## 26 March 2023 — GP escalation

The GP sends an urgent referral saying the pain is:

> **“causing death wish.”**

He writes:

> **“I am worried about him.”**

and:

> **“Despite his facade, I don’t think he is doing well. I’d be grateful for your review before we get into trouble here.”**

The March referral may therefore be an escalation of a warning already directly transmitted seven weeks earlier.

## 28 March 2023 — Neurology nursing corroboration

The Parkinson’s nurse records **“debilitating pain”** and **“no quality of life.”**

## Later 2023 — the explanatory conflict becomes visible inside Neurology

The neurologist records a **“misalignment”** and says it would be a fair characterisation of the patient’s view that the clinician held a **“firm belief that everything has to be explainable by the Parkinson’s”**, while describing the patient’s concerns about the feet as **“very reasonable and justifiable.”**

That is not a confession of legal anchoring.

It is something more useful:

> **The disagreement itself had become clinically visible.**

The same note acknowledges that it would be “embarrassing” if there were another cause while expressly saying that getting the diagnosis right mattered more than being personally right.

That does not prove bad faith.

## 2025 — renewed life-tolerability language

The Neurology note records **“murder in the feet”**, the first 150 footsteps as **“torture”**, and:

> **“I won’t live with it.”**

The record shows substantial neurological and pain management.

The precise documentary question is:

> **What structured safeguarding response, if any, corresponded to the life-safety signal itself?**

No documented safeguarding bridge has yet been identified in the reviewed corpus. That is a documentary negative, not proof that no undocumented response occurred.

The expert question is:

> **What did reasonable care require once severe pain, life-tolerability language, an urgent GP “death wish” warning and an acknowledged explanatory misalignment existed at the same time?**

# 6. Why 2023 changes the whole case

The 2023 crisis is not merely a later damages chapter.

It changes the architecture because it:

1. provides documented notice independent of the Protest;
2. joins unresolved pain to an explicit safety concern;
3. joins the safety concern to an acknowledged dispute about the explanatory model;
4. creates a second decision-after-notice case.

So even if the Protest is rejected, the case still has to answer:

> **By 2023 the service had documentary notice that the pain had become dangerous to the patient’s ability to endure life with it, while Neurology itself recognised that doctor and patient were materially misaligned about what the problem meant. What did reasonable care require then?**

The Protest makes the case earlier.

The 2023 documents make the mechanism harder to deny.

# 7. The democratic case-selection and teaching test

The repository should preserve one question outside the legal framework:

> **What case do the patient’s family and friends think this is after hearing the evidence fairly?**

Their answer is not evidence of breach.

It does not decide causation.

It does not prove the Protest.

Its purpose is **case selection, comprehension and teaching**.

The case has become technically dense. A good architecture should still be understandable by an ordinary person who knows the patient but is not a neurologist or lawyer.

Give family and friends three versions.

### Version A — the documentary case

> **He went to Neurology with disabling painful feet inside a gait-and-stiffness problem. Neurology found a very abnormal neurological leg, but the record does not clearly show what work established that the feet could be treated as separate.**

Ask:

> **Is that already the case you think should be taken? Why?**

### Version B — add the Protest

> **Before discharge he says he told them they had not dealt with his feet, while they still had the opportunity to examine, explain, reassure, retain the problem, share ownership or document the disagreement.**

Ask:

> **Does that change what you think the case is about? Why?**

### Version C — add the 2023 second fork

> **By 2023 he had directly written that the foot pain was perfect torture and that he did not think he could live with it anymore; the GP then urgently wrote that the pain was causing a death wish and that he was worried; Neurology recorded no quality of life; and later the neurologist recorded that doctor and patient were misaligned about the explanation.**

Ask:

> **Does this make the case mainly about the founding examination, what happened after notice, safety, or all three as one longitudinal problem?**

Do not use this as a popularity vote.

Use it to see whether the case teaches itself.

If ordinary people consistently identify the same gravitational centre, that is valuable drafting feedback.

If they misunderstand it, the architecture is not yet clear enough.

If they divide between the no-Protest and with-Protest cases, that helps identify where the real evidential hinge sits.

The live question should remain:

> **Which case would an ordinary person take after hearing each version fairly, and what fact made the difference?**

That is an opportunity to teach the case before law or expert terminology obscures it.

# 8. Teach the case in five minutes

Do not begin with PRKN, Dunne, HIQA, neuropathy, biomechanics or the examination audit.

Teach it in this order:

### Lesson 1 — what entered Neurology

> **Painful feet + abnormal gait + stiffness/tone + movement difficulty.**

### Lesson 2 — what Neurology found

> **A profound movement disorder and a lower limb that looked dystonic rather than weak.**

### Lesson 3 — what happened to the pain question

> **The source remained unresolved, but the feet were allowed to become possibly separate / neuropathic / Not clear.**

### Lesson 4 — what changes if the Protest is true

> **The patient pointed at the unfinished problem before discharge and the service still had several responsible futures available.**

### Lesson 5 — what 2023 independently proves

> **Years later the same pain had become a safety problem, and the explanatory disagreement was explicitly visible inside Neurology.**

Then ask:

> **What do you think the case is?**

Only after that answer should medicine and law become more technical.

The teaching principle is:

> **If the case cannot be understood before the law is named, the law will not save it.**

# 9. The record-absorption rule

Every new record must be forced through the three-argument architecture.

Ask:

1. Does it change the founding integrated presentation?
2. Does it show the relationship-directed examination actually occurred?
3. Does it earn separation?
4. Does it weaken separation?
5. Does it show notice?
6. Does it show response after notice?
7. Does it identify ownership?
8. Does it change the safety chronology?
9. Does it alter causation?
10. Is it merely another label?

A later note does not defeat the architecture simply because it is later or sophisticated.

> **The case does not become clearer by volume. It becomes clearer when every record is forced to answer the same questions about pain, relationship, notice, choice, ownership, safety and consequence.**

# 12. The legal structure stays underneath

The language becomes simpler. The legal structure does not.

Possible breach questions are date-specific:

1. whether enough was done in 2017 to answer the foot–neurology relationship;
2. whether complete separation was adequately supported;
3. what should have happened if the Protest occurred;
4. who owned the Neurology–Podiatry relationship;
5. what should have happened after medication crossed the supposed boundary;
6. when later evidence required reconstruction;
7. what reasonable care required in February–March 2023 when severe pain and life-safety language entered the treating network;
8. what the acknowledged explanatory misalignment required;
9. what safeguarding response, if any, was required;
10. what difference better care probably would have made.

The Irish-law spotlight is:

> **information available → choices available → course actually followed → professional standard → probable consequence.**

Do not substitute motive for this sequence.
