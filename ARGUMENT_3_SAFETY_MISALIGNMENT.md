# 1. The proposition

> **By 2023 the unresolved pain problem had become a safety problem, and at the same time Neurology itself documented that doctor and patient were materially misaligned about the explanatory model governing that pain.**

This argument does not depend on the 2017 Protest.

It is documentarily established through:

- a direct patient email;
- an urgent GP referral;
- a Parkinson’s nurse email;
- the neurologist’s own clinic letter;
- later Neurology wording in 2025.

The question is:

> **What did reasonable care require once severe pain, life-tolerability language, clinician-authored safety concern and an acknowledged explanatory misalignment existed at the same time?**

---

# 2. 1 February 2023 — direct patient notice

On 1 February 2023 the patient wrote directly to Thomas Monaghan, Patrick Browne and Aaron Brennan.

The email is not a later reconstruction.

It is timestamped contemporaneous evidence of what was sent into the treating network.

The patient wrote:

> **“The hardest part of the entire experience was the foot pain.”**

He said that after the worst pain had been dulled by Lyrica, the residual pain was:

> **“absolutely intolerable though not unbearable.”**

Then:

> **“I have no idea how I tolerated the pain, because I don’t think I could live with it anymore. It is perfect torture.”**

He asked to increase Lyrica and also wanted further assessment of the broader neurological situation.

This email proves:

- the patient sent the message;
- it was addressed to the named recipients;
- he identified foot pain as the hardest part of the entire experience;
- he used direct life-tolerability language.

It does **not** by itself prove:

- that every recipient read it;
- when it was opened;
- suicidal intent;
- a suicide plan;
- that no oral response occurred.

The correct question is:

> **What should an ordinary competent recipient have done with that wording in this clinical context?**

---

# 3. 26 March 2023 — the GP escalates the warning

Seven weeks later the GP sends an **Urgent** Healthlink referral to Neurology.

The referral records severe bilateral burning pain in feet and lower legs and says:

> **“Says extreme at time and causing death wish.”**

The GP then writes:

> **“I am worried about him.”**

and:

> **“Despite his facade, I don’t think he is doing well. I’d be grateful for your review before we get into trouble here.”**

This matters because the March referral should not be read in isolation.

The GP was one of the recipients of the 1 February email.

The March referral may therefore represent an **escalation of a warning already directly transmitted into the clinical network**.

The evidential status has also changed.

The February wording is the patient’s own account.

The March wording is clinician-authored urgent medical communication.

The question becomes:

> **What response should a specialist service make when an urgent GP referral frames severe pain as causing a death wish and explicitly says “I am worried about him” and “before we get into trouble here”?**

That is an expert question.

---

# 4. 28 March 2023 — Neurology nursing corroborates severity

Two days later the Parkinson’s nurse writes to the consultant after speaking to the patient.

The message records:

> **“debilitating pain”**

and:

> **“no quality of life”**

with worsening over months.

The patient asks to be reviewed.

This provides a third independent documentary layer in the same crisis period:

1. patient: **“I don’t think I could live with it anymore”**;
2. GP: **“death wish” / “I am worried about him”**;
3. Neurology nurse: **“debilitating pain” / “no quality of life.”**

By this point the severity is no longer inferential.

It is explicitly present in the clinical communications.

---

# 5. The record does show clinical action

This argument must not be misstated as:

> nobody did anything.

The record shows real activity.

It includes:

- Lyrica treatment;
- attempts to obtain review;
- cancellation-list placement;
- Parkinson’s medication adjustment;
- later Stalevo introduction;
- consideration of OFF-related pain and shuffling;
- consideration of dystonia and neuropathy;
- later Fabry testing;
- substantial neurological review.

The stronger and more exact proposition is:

> **The system continued to manage pain and Neurology. What is not yet visible in the reviewed corpus is a structured safeguarding response corresponding to the life-safety signal itself.**

That distinction is load-bearing.

# 6. The safeguarding bridge

As of the current review, the canonical corpus has not yielded an identified:

- formal suicide-risk assessment;
- documented clarification of what “death wish” meant as a risk statement;
- documented safety plan;
- crisis pathway;
- liaison-psychiatry referral specifically linked to the 2023 pain crisis;
- clearly designated safeguarding owner;
- documented communication back to the GP describing a completed safeguarding response.

This is a **documentary negative**.

Do not say:

> no safeguarding occurred.

Say:

> **No documented safeguarding bridge has yet been identified in the reviewed corpus.**

The expert/disclosure questions are:

1. What did the 1 February email require?
2. Did the wording “I don’t think I could live with it anymore” require direct clarification?
3. What did the March “death wish” referral require?
4. Was a formal risk assessment clinically indicated?
5. Who owned the safety issue?
6. What was the patient told?
7. What was the GP told?
8. Was follow-up timed according to risk?
9. Was mental-health input indicated?
10. What harm, if any, followed from delay or absence of a safeguarding response?

# 7. The later 2023 clinic note changes the explanatory case

The neurologist later documents an explicit:

> **“misalignment”**

between doctor and patient.

He says it would be a fair characterisation of the patient’s view that the clinician held a:

> **“firm belief that everything has to be explainable by the Parkinson’s”**

while the patient had:

> **“very reasonable and justifiable concerns”**

that much related to the feet as a separate problem.

The same passage records that the first symptoms were in the feet and that the previous peripheral-neuropathy question had seemed to subside.

This is not a confession of legal anchoring.

It does not prove that the clinician literally believed every symptom had to be Parkinson’s.

It proves something narrower and more useful:

> **The explanatory conflict itself had become visible inside Neurology.**

That means the service could no longer be described as unaware that the patient regarded the governing model as inadequate.

---

# 8. The “embarrassing” passage

The same 2023 note records:

> **“While it would be embarrassing if there was a cause other than iPD, we have a much much larger interest in his diagnosis being right as opposed to me being right.”**

This must be handled carefully.

It does **not** prove:

- bad faith;
- pride caused the clinical course;
- deliberate refusal to reconsider.

Its significance is contextual.

The clinician himself recognises that personal/professional embarrassment at being wrong is a conceivable human factor, while expressly saying diagnostic truth matters more.

The safe proposition is:

> **The record itself acknowledges the human possibility of investment in an established explanation while simultaneously documenting that the patient’s contrary concerns were reasonable and justifiable.**

That is relevant to the importance of structured reassessment, not to motive.

# 9. Why misalignment and safety belong in the same argument

The most important point is that the pain crisis and the explanatory dispute are not separate chapters.

They coexist.

By the same broad period:

- the patient says the pain is intolerable;
- the GP reports “death wish” and says he is worried;
- the Parkinson’s nurse says “no quality of life”;
- the neurologist later records that doctor and patient are materially misaligned about what the problem means;
- the patient’s contrary concerns are described as reasonable and justifiable.

The question is therefore not simply:

> **Was the patient distressed?**

It is:

> **What did reasonable care require when the patient was in severe danger from a pain problem whose explanatory status was itself visibly contested?**

That is the second fork.

# 10. The second choice-space

Once the safety signal became explicit, a new range of possible futures opened.

These could include, depending on expert evidence and the actual clinical circumstances:

- direct clarification of life-tolerability language;
- suicide/self-harm risk assessment if indicated;
- urgent review;
- crisis/safety advice;
- GP coordination;
- named safeguarding ownership;
- mental-health or liaison-psychiatry involvement if indicated;
- intensified pain assessment;
- renewed movement-disorders assessment;
- reconsideration of OFF dystonia/neuropathic/mechanical/mixed mechanisms;
- explicit documentation of why a safeguarding pathway was or was not necessary;
- defined follow-up interval;
- preservation of the safety concern in later records.

The point is not that every one of these was mandatory.

The point is:

> **The life-safety warning created another decision-after-notice event.**

The issue is not whether every phrase meant active suicidal intent.

The issue is:

> **What assessment was made of the meaning and risk attached to those repeated statements, and where is that safeguarding response documented?**

# 13. The “facade” problem

The GP writes:

> **“Despite his facade, I don’t think he is doing well.”**

That deserves careful treatment.

The patient has repeatedly been described as intelligent, articulate, insightful and able to function through severe symptoms.

Those strengths may make severity easier to underestimate.

The argument is not that apparent composure proves hidden suicidality.

It is:

> **Composure cannot substitute for direct assessment when the record already contains explicit life-safety language.**

The GP’s warning specifically cautions the receiving service against relying on appearance alone.

# 15. The Irish-law spotlight

The legal task is not to infer subjective motive.

The legal task is to test the course actually followed.

The structure is:

> **information available → safety notice → explanatory misalignment → reasonable options available → response actually demonstrated → standard of care → probable consequence.**

Relevant expert questions include:

- what risk assessment was required;
- whether Neurology could reasonably leave safeguarding to primary care;
- whether the urgency of review was adequate;
- whether the acknowledged misalignment required a more fundamental reassessment;
- whether any failure probably contributed to additional psychological harm or prolonged dangerous pain.

Causation must remain separate from breach.

> **Those facts make direct assessment and clear ownership more important, because apparent mood and clinical complexity cannot resolve what the explicit safety language meant.**
