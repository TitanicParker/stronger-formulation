# Three-Argument Architecture

**Status:** CONTROLLING ARGUMENT MAP

**Purpose:** Give the repository one gravitational centre, three separate argument tracks, and a democratic way to test whether the case can be understood before legal doctrine is applied.

**Controlling factual source:** `SRC_COMPLETE_RECORD_FORENSIC.md`

---

# 1. The gravitational pull

This is one longitudinal case, not three unrelated allegations.

The centre is:

> **Unresolved source of disabling pain**  
> **becomes specialist uncertainty about a possibly separate foot problem.**

Then the record keeps returning to the same territory.

The feet change with neurological treatment. The GP later puts feet, gait and tone back together. Neurology later considers OFF dystonia and OFF-related pain. By 2023 the pain has become so severe that the patient says he does not think he can live with it anymore, the GP writes “death wish” and “I am worried about him”, the Parkinson’s nurse records “no quality of life”, and Neurology itself records an explanatory “misalignment”.

The case therefore has **three arguments**.

They are not rivals.

They are nested.

Argument 1 survives if the Protest is rejected.

Argument 2 is what Argument 1 becomes if the Protest is established.

Argument 3 is independently documentary and changes the longitudinal meaning of both.

The master question is:

> **What happened each time the patient, the GP, treatment response or the clinical record itself showed that the existing explanation was no longer enough for the problem the patient was actually living with?**

---

# 2. Argument One — The Founding Separation

**Question:**

> **What work earned the separation?**

The patient did not arrive with two settled problems.

He arrived with painful abnormal feet, abnormal gait, increased tone/stiffness, movement-initiation difficulty, toe clawing and declining function.

Neurology correctly identified severe young-onset Parkinsonism. It also identified a striking gait and a lower-limb appearance suggestive of dystonic posture rather than true foot drop.

Structural pathology was real. Podiatry was appropriate.

But structural pathology is not structural independence.

The surviving record does not clearly show the bridge: the examination that established how the abnormal neurological lower limb related to the painful structural feet before the feet were allowed to sit as something that “may have been completely separate”, possibly neuropathic, and “Not clear”.

This is the strongest case without the Protest.

It asks:

> **They saw the abnormal neurological walk. They knew the feet hurt. Where is the work showing why the two were separate?**

This argument can stand even if the Protest is rejected completely.

---

# 3. Argument Two — The Protest and the Disclosed Choice

**Conditional on establishing the Protest as evidence.**

The Protest is not important merely because the patient complained.

Its importance is that it **restores the choice**.

The patient says, in substance:

> **You have not dealt with my feet.**

If that is established, the case changes.

Before the Protest the question is whether the unfinished problem was appreciated.

After the Protest the question is:

> **What did the service choose to do once the patient had pointed directly at the unfinished source of disabling pain?**

The available futures were not binary.

The service could have:

- re-examined fully;
- done a limited targeted return to the painful feet;
- asked for senior or movement-disorders review;
- asked Podiatry a defined neurological/biomechanical question;
- kept the causal relationship expressly open;
- reassured the patient that the pain remained owned;
- arranged a return review;
- documented disagreement;
- transferred structural treatment to Podiatry while Neurology retained responsibility for the relationship;
- or, at the absolute minimum, preserved the Protest in the portable record.

The point is not that one particular response was automatically mandatory.

The point is that **many clinically and documentarily responsible futures were still available**.

The Protest is therefore:

> **the widest point in the future.**

Everything after it is loss of optionality.

If the Protest occurred and did not travel in the specialist account, its omission does not merely remove a quotation.

> **It removes the fork from history.**

The later reader sees the outcome — possibly separate / neuropathy / Not clear / Podiatry — without seeing that closure had been contested while another course was still available.

That is the spotlight under Irish law: not motive, not secret intention, but:

> **information available → options available → course actually followed → professional standard → consequence.**

# 4. Argument Three — The Second Fork: Pain, Safety and Misalignment, 2023–2025

This argument does not depend on the Protest.

By 2023 the unresolved pain problem has changed status.

It is no longer only a source-of-pain question.

It has become a safety question.

## 1 February 2023 — direct patient notice

The patient emails Thomas Monaghan, Patrick Browne and Aaron Brennan.

He says the foot pain was:

> **“The hardest part of the entire experience.”**

He says the residual pain is:

> **“absolutely intolerable though not unbearable.”**

Then:

> **“I have no idea how I tolerated the pain, because I don’t think I could live with it anymore. It is perfect torture.”**

This is direct notice into the treating network.

It does not by itself prove suicidal intent. It does prove that the patient had put the tolerability of continued life with the pain into words.

## 26 March 2023 — GP escalation

The GP sends an urgent referral stating that the pain is:

> **“causing death wish.”**

He writes:

> **“I am worried about him.”**

and:

> **“Despite his facade, I don’t think he is doing well. I’d be grateful for your review before we get into trouble here.”**

The March referral is therefore not merely the first safety signal.

It may be the escalation of a warning already directly transmitted seven weeks earlier.

## 28 March 2023 — Neurology nursing corroboration

The Parkinson’s nurse records:

> **“debilitating pain”**

and:

> **“no quality of life.”**

## Later 2023 — the explanatory conflict becomes visible inside Neurology

The neurologist records a:

> **“misalignment”**

and says it would be a fair characterisation of the patient’s view that the clinician held a:

> **“firm belief that everything has to be explainable by the Parkinson’s”**

while describing the patient’s concerns about the feet as:

> **“very reasonable and justifiable.”**

This is not a confession of legal anchoring.

It is something more useful:

> **The disagreement itself had become clinically visible.**

The same note acknowledges that it would be “embarrassing” if there were another cause while expressly saying that getting the diagnosis right mattered more than being personally right.

That does not prove bad faith.

It shows that professional investment in an existing explanation was understood as a human possibility inside the consultation itself.

## 2025 — the same life-tolerability language is inside Neurology’s own record

The Neurology note records:

> **“murder in the feet”**

> **the first 150 footsteps are “torture”**

and:

> **“I won’t live with it.”**

The question is no longer whether the pain was visible.

The question is:

> **What structured safeguarding response, if any, corresponded to the life-safety signal itself?**

The current corpus shows substantial neurological and pain management.

It does **not yet show a documented safeguarding bridge** equivalent in visibility to the warning language.

That is a documentary negative, not proof that no undocumented response occurred.

The expert question is:

> **What did reasonable care require once severe pain, life-tolerability language, an urgent GP “death wish” warning and an acknowledged explanatory misalignment existed at the same time?**

# 5. Why the third argument changes the whole case

The 2023 crisis is not a damages appendix.

It changes the architecture.

It does four things.

First, it gives the case **documented notice** independent of the Protest.

Second, it joins the unresolved pain to an explicit safety concern.

Third, it joins that safety concern to an acknowledged dispute about the explanatory model.

Fourth, it creates a second decision-after-notice case.

So even if a fact-finder rejects the 2017 Protest entirely, the case still has to answer this:

> **By 2023 the service unquestionably had documentary notice that the pain had become dangerous to the patient’s ability to endure life with it, while Neurology itself recognised that doctor and patient were materially misaligned about what the problem meant. What did reasonable care require then?**

The Protest makes the case earlier.

The 2023 documents make the mechanism harder to deny.

# 6. The democratic case-selection test

The project should keep one question deliberately outside the legal language:

> **What case do the people who know the patient best think this is?**

That means family and friends.

Their view is **not evidence of breach**. It does not replace expert evidence. It does not decide causation. It does not prove the Protest. It does not establish the legal case.

Its value is different.

It is a comprehension and case-selection test.

The case has become technically dense over many years. Neurology, Podiatry, Parkinsonism, dystonia, neuropathy, treatment response, governance, safety and genetics can all pull the reader toward specialist detail.

Family and friends can be asked a simpler democratic question:

> **When you hear the case told accurately, what do you think the case is actually about?**

Give them three versions without telling them which one is preferred.

### Version A — the document-only founding case

> **He went to Neurology with disabling painful feet inside a gait-and-stiffness problem. Neurology found a very abnormal neurological leg but the record does not clearly show what work established that the feet could be treated as separate.**

Ask:

> **Is that already the case?**

### Version B — the same case with the Protest

Add:

> **Before discharge he says he told them they had not dealt with his feet, while they still had the opportunity to examine, explain, reassure, retain the problem or document the disagreement.**

Ask:

> **Does that change what you think the case is about?**

### Version C — the later documented second fork

Add the 2023 sequence:

> **By 2023 he had directly written that the foot pain was perfect torture and that he did not think he could live with it anymore; the GP then urgently wrote that the pain was causing a death wish and that he was worried; Neurology recorded no quality of life; and later the neurologist recorded that doctor and patient were misaligned about the explanation.**

Ask:

> **Does this make the case mainly about the original foot examination, about what happened after notice, about safety, or about all three as one longitudinal problem?**

This is not a poll to decide litigation.

It is a way to test whether the architecture communicates the truth of the record without requiring the listener to become a neurologist or lawyer.

If family and friends repeatedly understand the same gravitational centre, that is useful drafting feedback.

If they consistently misunderstand the case, the drafting has failed.

If they divide between the no-Protest and with-Protest versions, that is useful too. It shows where the evidential hinge actually sits.

The democratic test should therefore be preserved as a live question:

> **Which case would an ordinary person take after hearing each version fairly, and why?**

The answer is not binding.

The reasoning is valuable.

# 7. Teach the case through the choice

The case should be teachable in five minutes.

Do not begin with PRKN, Dunne, HIQA, neuropathy or the 50-item examination audit.

Teach it in this order:

### Lesson 1 — what entered the hospital

> **Painful feet + abnormal gait + stiffness/tone + movement difficulty.**

### Lesson 2 — what Neurology found

> **A profound movement disorder and a lower limb that looked dystonic rather than weak.**

### Lesson 3 — what happened to the pain question

> **The source remained unresolved, but the feet were allowed to become possibly separate / neuropathic / Not clear.**

### Lesson 4 — what changes if the Protest is true

> **The patient pointed at the unfinished problem before discharge and the service still had multiple responsible futures available.**

### Lesson 5 — what 2023 proves independently

> **Years later the same pain had become a safety problem, and the explanatory disagreement was now explicitly visible inside Neurology.**

Then ask the listener:

> **What do you think the case is?**

Only after they answer should the legal framework be introduced.

This is the teaching principle:

> **If the case cannot be understood before the law is named, the law will not save it.**

# 8. The record-absorption rule

Every new record must be forced through this architecture.

Ask:

1. Does it change the founding integrated presentation?
2. Does it show a relationship-directed examination actually occurred?
3. Does it earn separation?
4. Does it weaken separation?
5. Does it show notice?
6. Does it show response after notice?
7. Does it identify ownership?
8. Does it change the safety chronology?
9. Does it alter causation?
10. Is it merely another label?

A later note does not defeat this architecture merely because it is later or sophisticated.

A new label does not become a new case simply by accumulating in the record.

> **The case does not become clearer by volume. It becomes clearer when every record is forced to answer the same questions about pain, relationship, notice, choice, ownership, safety and consequence.**

# 11. Irish-law spotlight

The law comes last.

The project should expose the choices first.

Under the Irish clinical-negligence framework, including the Dunne principles, the core expert question is:

> **Was the course actually followed one that an ordinarily careful practitioner of equivalent status and skill could properly have followed on the information then available?**

The Protest matters because, if established, it discloses an earlier decision-after-notice.

The 2023 record matters because it creates a later documented decision-after-notice whether or not the Protest is accepted.

The legal task is not to prove motive.

It is to compare:

> **information available → choices available → course actually followed → standard of care → probable consequence.**
