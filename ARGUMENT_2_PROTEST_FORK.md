# Argument 2 — The Protest and the Disclosed Choice

**Status:** CONDITIONAL DECISION-AFTER-NOTICE CASE  
**Depends on the Protest:** Yes  
**Controlling source:** `SRC_COMPLETE_RECORD_FORENSIC.md` plus later patient-authored Protest material

---

# 1. The proposition

> **If the Protest is established, the case changes from possible omission to decision after express notice.**

The patient says, in substance:

> **You have not dealt with my feet.**

The Protest does not prove dystonia.

It does not prove that every foot pain episode was neurological.

It does not prove negligence by itself.

Its importance is different:

> **It identifies the precise moment at which the patient tells the service that the central painful problem has not, in his perception, been dealt with while the original phenotype is still physically present and correction remains possible.**

That is the fork.

---

# 2. Why the Protest matters

Before the Protest, one possible explanation is that the unresolved feet–pain relationship had simply not yet been appreciated.

After the Protest, that explanation becomes much harder.

The service has now been told:

> **the painful feet remain unresolved.**

The question is no longer only:

> **Did they miss the unfinished problem?**

It becomes:

> **What did they do once the patient pointed directly at it?**

The Protest therefore converts:

> **possible pre-existing omission**

into:

> **express notice + continuing opportunity + a choice of response.**

---

# 3. The Protest is the widest point in the future

The answer after the Protest did not have to be a diagnosis.

The answer did not have to be PRKN.

The answer did not have to be dystonia.

The answer did not have to be levodopa.

The answer did not even have to be a repeat full examination.

There were many clinically, relationally and documentarily responsible futures still available.

They included:

1. **Immediate full reopening** — shoes/socks off, pain mapping, plantar/dynamic examination, repeat gait and baseline capture.
2. **Limited repeat examination** — a brief targeted return specifically to the painful feet.
3. **Senior or movement-disorders review** — examine the dystonic-looking lower limb and painful feet together.
4. **Joint Neurology–Podiatry question** — send Podiatry a defined neurological/biomechanical question rather than only a body part.
5. **Active diagnostic uncertainty** — state expressly that the pain could be neurological, mechanical, neuropathic or mixed and remains open.
6. **Acknowledgement and reassurance** — tell the patient that the pain has been heard, is not dismissed and remains owned even if the answer is not yet known.
7. **Planned therapeutic observation** — preserve baseline and review what treatment changes.
8. **Planned outpatient return** — no prolonged admission required, but a defined return specifically for the feet–pain–gait relationship.
9. **Legitimate separation after directed assessment** — examine adequately and conclude that structural/neuropathic mechanisms are more probable.
10. **Disagree but document disagreement** — record that the patient believes the feet have not been dealt with and explain the clinician’s assessment and plan.
11. **Transfer practical care while retaining neurological ownership** — Podiatry treats structure while Neurology retains responsibility for causal integration.
12. **Do nothing further clinically but preserve the Protest** — future clinicians still know that closure was contested.
13. **Closure plus omission** — no demonstrated return; possible separation/neuropathy/Not clear/Podiatry; Protest does not travel.

The point is not that one of the first twelve was automatically mandatory.

The point is:

> **The Protest reveals how many responsible futures remained available before the record narrowed into one surviving path.**

---

# 4. The fork is multi-axis, not binary

The Protest creates a decision matrix.

## Clinical axis

re-examine ↔ do not re-examine

## Epistemic axis

keep causation open ↔ treat separation as sufficiently settled

## Ownership axis

Neurology retains/shared ownership ↔ feet/pain become someone else’s practical problem

## Relational axis

acknowledge/reassure ↔ patient leaves believing the central painful problem remains undealt with

## Documentary axis

record Protest/response ↔ Protest does not travel

## Longitudinal axis

planned return loop ↔ no defined return loop

These axes can combine.

A service can decide not to re-examine immediately yet still preserve uncertainty, reassure the patient, retain ownership and arrange review.

A service can re-examine and still legitimately conclude separation.

That is why the defence cannot reduce the case to:

> **Was an elaborate repeat foot examination mandatory?**

The counterfactual includes much less demanding professional responses.

> **Documentation was itself one available treatment for uncertainty.**

Not treatment of pain.

Treatment of the information problem.

---

# 5. What the surviving record appears to show

Describe the actual documented branch neutrally.

Things that happened/documented:

- Podiatry existed and was pursued;
- sophisticated neurological synthesis occurred;
- uncertainty was documented;
- possible separation was documented;
- possible neuropathy was documented;
- the dystonic-looking lower-limb observation survived elsewhere in the neurological record.

Things not demonstrated in the surviving record:

- a relationship-directed post-Protest foot examination;
- an explicit reassurance/retained-ownership response;
- a planned return specifically to the source of disabling pain;
- preservation of the Protest in the formal specialist account.

Do not turn “not demonstrated” into proof that none of those events happened.

The proper formulation is:

> **The surviving record reflects a course in which the dystonic-looking neurological observation remains, the feet receive a practical Podiatry destination, and the later specialist synthesis says possibly separate / neuropathy / Not clear, while the Protest itself does not travel.**

---

# 6. The omission of the Protest changes the information state

This is one of the most important distinctions in the case.

There is a difference between:

> **Feet remain unclear; Podiatry referral arranged.**

and:

> **Feet remain unclear; before discharge the patient expressly said that the principal painful presenting problem had not been dealt with.**

The first looks like managed uncertainty.

The second looks like contested closure.

If the Protest occurred and was omitted from the outward specialist account, the omission does not merely remove something the patient said.

> **It changes what downstream clinicians know about the status of the uncertainty.**

The GP can no longer see that the patient had disputed the completeness of the specialist assessment before discharge.

The later reader sees the specialist outcome without seeing the choice that preceded it.

> **The omission of the Protest removes the fork from the historical record.**

---

# 7. Why the patient’s own distinction does not resolve the Protest

The specialist account records that the patient had “markedly divorced” the bodily tension from the foot pain.

That may be true.

It does not answer the Protest.

A patient can say:

> these sensations feel different

and still say:

> you have not investigated what is causing the painful feet.

Phenomenological difference is not mechanistic independence.

The Protest is therefore not contradicted by the patient’s own sensory distinction.

It concerns **whether the source and relationship were dealt with**, not whether the patient thought two sensations felt identical.

---

# 8. The dystonic-looking lower limb becomes an orphaned observation

The important neurological sign was not missed.

It was seen.

It was characterised.

It was useful enough to enter the diagnostic imagination.

The unresolved question is:

> **If that limb looked dystonic, what was it doing to the painful feet on which the patient had to stand and walk?**

If the Protest is established, the patient effectively asks the service to return to that relationship before closure.

If no demonstrated return occurred, the dystonic-looking lower limb becomes an **orphaned high-value observation**: clinically important, preserved in the record, but not visibly joined to the source-of-pain synthesis.

---

# 9. Expertise increases the significance of the opportunity

The proper argument is not:

> every clinician knew PRKN.

The proper argument is:

> **the service had access to substantial neurological expertise, had already recognised an unusually young and atypical Parkinsonian syndrome, had a complex-case environment, and had identified the dystonic-looking lower limb.**

Institutional capability is not personal knowledge.

But it matters to the opportunity question.

At the Protest, the service still had:

- the patient;
- the painful feet;
- the abnormal gait;
- the dystonic-looking lower limb;
- structural deformity;
- video capability;
- Neurology expertise;
- Podiatry access;
- Physio/OT involvement;
- the ability to document uncertainty;
- the ability to arrange a return.

The Protest is therefore the widest point in the future because the original phenotype is still available.

> **You can reread a note. You cannot put the 2017 patient back in the room.**

---

# 10. Loss of optionality

After the Protest, options narrow with time.

Discharge removes immediate access to the untreated/minimally treated phenotype.

Later synthesis becomes the portable account.

The GP inherits specialist uncertainty.

Podiatry treats the structural problem.

Treatment changes the baseline.

Years of adaptation, pain and medication make later reconstruction harder.

The longitudinal argument is therefore:

> **The Protest preserves the counterfactual.**

It identifies the last moment at which the service could still choose among many futures while the raw phenomenon remained available.

---

# 11. The Irish-law spotlight

The Protest should not be used to prove motive.

The case does not need to prove that clinicians consciously chose closure for self-protection, pride or convenience.

The legally cleaner sequence is:

> **information available → express notice → reasonable options available → course actually followed → professional standard → consequence.**

The expert question is:

> **Assuming the Protest occurred, what would ordinary competent Neurology have been expected to do next?**

Possible answers include examination, explanation, senior review, retained ownership, documentation or follow-up.

The court or expert can then compare those possibilities with the course actually demonstrated in the record.

---

# 12. The strongest defence

The defence remains:

- the neurological diagnosis was profound and complex;
- structural foot disease was real;
- Podiatry was appropriate;
- the patient himself distinguished the pain from bodily tension;
- the service documented uncertainty rather than false certainty;
- there is no proof that no conversation or re-examination occurred;
- no single post-Protest response may have been mandatory.

The response is:

> **The enormity of the Protest does not lie in proving one particular response was mandatory. It lies in showing how many clinically, humanly and documentarily responsible futures remained open after the patient identified the unresolved source of his disabling pain.**

---

# 13. The one-line argument

> **The purpose of establishing the Protest is not merely to prove that the patient complained. It is to restore to the historical record the choice that immediately followed his complaint.**
