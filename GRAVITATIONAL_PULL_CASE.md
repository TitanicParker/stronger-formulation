# 0. What this document does

This document does not ask the reader to choose between three unrelated cases.

It identifies **one longitudinal case with three separable propositions**, two of which survive without the disputed Protest and one of which becomes materially stronger if the Protest is established.

The master question is now:

> **What happened each time information supplied by the patient, the GP, treatment response or the clinical record itself showed that the operative explanation was no longer sufficient for the problem the patient was actually living with?**

The case has two major forks.

The first is in 2017.

The second is documentarily established in 2023.

The first may depend partly on patient evidence.

The second does not.

The consequence is strategic:

> **The Protest is no longer the single point on which the case turns. It is the earliest possible proof that the central mechanism was already present. The 2023 record independently demonstrates the same mechanism under conditions of explicit pain crisis, direct notice, recognised explanatory misalignment and a safeguarding question.**

The strongest case **without** the Protest is therefore much stronger than a simple 2017 documentation case.

The strongest case **with** the Protest is the same longitudinal case, but with an earlier moment of express notice and an earlier disclosed choice.

# 1. The gravitational centre

The original repository centre remains correct:

> **Unresolved source of disabling pain**  
> **becomes**  
> **specialist uncertainty about a possibly separate foot problem.**

But the later record now supplies a second transformation:

> **Unresolved source of disabling pain**  
> **becomes a life-safety problem**  
> **while the explanatory model governing that pain is itself expressly recognised as misaligned.**

The whole case can therefore be stated in one sentence:

> **A patient entered Neurology with disabling painful feet inside an integrated feet–gait–tone–movement problem; the source of that pain was allowed to become specialist uncertainty about a possibly separate foot problem; later neurological treatment repeatedly crossed that separation; and by 2023 the same unresolved pain had become severe enough to generate direct life-tolerability language, an urgent GP “death wish” warning, a nursing “no quality of life” warning, and an explicit neurological record of misalignment about the explanatory model—without a documented safeguarding bridge yet identified in the surviving corpus.**

That is the gravitational pull.

Every record must now answer one or more of these questions:

1. **What does this record tell us about the source of the disabling pain?**
2. **Does it support, weaken or require reconsideration of separation between feet and neurological motor state?**
3. **What did the clinician or service know at that date?**
4. **Did the patient, GP or another clinician expressly challenge the operative model?**
5. **What choices remained open after that challenge?**
6. **Who owned the unresolved problem after the encounter?**
7. **What information travelled forward?**
8. **What information did not travel?**
9. **Did treatment change the feet, pain, gait or function?**
10. **Did the record reach a safety threshold requiring a different kind of response?**
11. **What would reasonable care probably have changed?**
12. **What harm would have happened anyway?**

A record that does not alter one of those questions is peripheral.

A record does not become important merely because it is later, longer or more sophisticated.

# 2. One case, three propositions

## Proposition A — The founding separation

**This proposition requires no Protest.**

The documentary case is:

- primary care sent an integrated problem involving painful abnormal feet, gait disturbance, increased tone/stiffness, movement-initiation difficulty, toe clawing and declining function;
- Neurology correctly identified severe young-onset Parkinsonism;
- Neurology itself recorded a strikingly abnormal gait and a foot-drop-like lower-limb appearance suggestive of dystonic posture rather than true weakness;
- structural foot disease was real;
- the surviving record does not clearly demonstrate the examination that established how the abnormal neurological lower limb related to the painful structural feet;
- the later specialist synthesis nevertheless allowed the feet to sit as something that “may have been completely separate,” possibly neuropathic, and “Not clear.”

The question is:

> **What work earned the separation?**

This is the founding clinical case.

It survives if every word of the Protest is rejected.

## Proposition B — The 2017 fork

**This proposition depends on establishing the Protest as evidence.**

The patient says that before discharge he said, in substance:

> **You have not dealt with my feet.**

The Protest does not prove a mechanism.

It does not prove dystonia.

It does not prove negligence.

It does something more precise:

> **It converts a possible omission into express notice while the original phenotype is still physically available for correction.**

Once the Protest is established, the question changes from:

> **Did they appreciate the unfinished pain problem?**

to:

> **What did reasonable care require once the patient expressly told the service that the painful problem had not been dealt with?**

The possible futures were not binary.

They included:

- immediate full reopening;
- limited targeted foot examination;
- senior neurological review;
- movement-disorders review;
- defined Neurology–Podiatry collaboration;
- explicit active uncertainty;
- reassurance without immediate reinvestigation;
- planned therapeutic observation;
- planned outpatient return;
- legitimate separation after directed assessment;
- disagreement with the patient but documentation of that disagreement;
- transfer of structural treatment to Podiatry while Neurology retained causal ownership;
- no further clinical work that day but preservation of the Protest in the portable record.

The Protest therefore identifies a **choice-space**.

Its omission from the portable account, if established, does not merely remove a sentence.

> **It removes the fork from the historical record.**

## Proposition C — The second fork: 2023–2025

**This proposition is documentarily established and independent of the Protest.**

By 2023 the unresolved pain problem has changed status.

It is no longer merely a source-of-pain question.

It has become a safety question.

The sequence is direct.

### 1 February 2023 — direct patient notice

The patient emails Thomas Monaghan, Patrick Browne and Aaron Brennan.

He says the foot pain was:

> **“The hardest part of the entire experience.”**

He says that after the worst had been dulled by Lyrica, the residual pain remained:

> **“absolutely intolerable though not unbearable.”**

He says:

> **“I have no idea how I tolerated the pain, because I don’t think I could live with it anymore. It is perfect torture.”**

**Source:** Gmail message `1860e71b17791905`, sent 1 February 2023.

This email proves direct transmission to the named addresses. It does not by itself prove when each recipient opened it or whether there was an undocumented oral response.

### 26 March 2023 — GP escalation

The GP sends an urgent Healthlink Neurology referral.

It records severe bilateral burning pain in feet and lower legs:

> **“Says extreme at time and causing death wish.”**

The GP writes:

> **“I am worried about him.”**

and:

> **“Despite his facade, I don’t think he is doing well. I’d be grateful for your review before we get into trouble here.”**

**Source:** `SRC_COMPLETE_RECORD_FORENSIC.md`, CLIN-002633–CLIN-002643.

The March referral therefore should not be analysed merely as the first appearance of psychological danger.

It may be an **escalation of a warning already transmitted directly into the treating network seven weeks earlier**.

### 28 March 2023 — Neurology nursing corroboration

The Parkinson’s nurse writes to the consultant after speaking to the patient:

> **“debilitating pain”**

and:

> **“no quality of life”**

with worsening over months.

**Source:** `SRC_COMPLETE_RECORD_FORENSIC.md`, CLIN-002677–CLIN-002687.

### Later 2023 — the explanatory conflict becomes explicit inside Neurology

The neurologist records what he says would be a fair characterisation of the patient’s view:

> there was a **“misalignment”** between them;

the patient would believe the clinician had a:

> **“firm belief that everything has to be explainable by the Parkinson’s”**

while the patient had:

> **“very reasonable and justifiable concerns”**

that much related to the feet as a separate problem.

The same passage records that the first symptoms were in the feet “all those years ago” and that the previous peripheral-neuropathy question had seemed to subside.

**Source:** `SRC_COMPLETE_RECORD_FORENSIC.md`, CLIN-002477–CLIN-002482.

This must be used carefully.

It is not a confession that the clinician was legally anchored.

It is the clinician recording a serious disagreement about the explanatory model and describing the patient’s alternative concern as reasonable and justifiable.

The same note also records:

> **“While it would be embarrassing if there was a cause other than iPD, we have a much much larger interest in his diagnosis being right as opposed to me being right.”**

That is not evidence of improper motive.

It is evidence that the clinician himself recognised the human possibility of personal or professional investment in an established diagnosis while expressly stating that diagnostic truth mattered more.

### 2025 — life-tolerability language is recorded by Neurology itself

The 2025 Neurology note records:

- the feet are the thing on the patient’s mind;
- he hobbles out of bed;
- the feet can be too sore to tie his runners;
- mornings feel like **“murder in the feet”**;
- the first 150 footsteps are **“torture”**;
- and:

> **“To say ‘I won’t live with it’ allows him put words on the situation.”**

**Source:** `SRC_COMPLETE_RECORD_FORENSIC.md`, CLIN-002768–CLIN-002800.

**Date caution:** the year-split extract does not itself print the exact clinic date. Other material in the corpus identifies the Sinemet-CR plan with 10 March 2025, while a later patient-authored “Staying alive” attachment labels an encounter 10 April 2025. The native clinic letter should control the final date/version.

The substantive point is not date-dependent:

> **Neurology itself eventually records the patient again describing the continuing pain in language of not being willing to live with it.**

# 4. The two forks

The longitudinal structure is best understood as two forks.

## Fork One — 2017: diagnostic ownership

The underlying question:

> **What is causing the disabling foot pain, and what does the neurological disorder have to do with it?**

Possible outcomes after notice include:

- investigate;
- explain;
- reassure;
- retain ownership;
- share ownership;
- document disagreement;
- preserve uncertainty;
- arrange return;
- legitimately separate after enough assessment.

The surviving record instead eventually contains:

> **possibly separate / possible neuropathy / Not clear / Podiatry**

while the patient’s alleged Protest does not appear in the formal specialist account.

The legal/expert question is not:

> **Why did they secretly choose closure?**

It is:

> **Was the course actually followed one that ordinary competent neurological care permitted on the information then available?**

## Fork Two — 2023: safety and explanatory adequacy

The underlying question is no longer only:

> **What causes the pain?**

It also becomes:

> **How close is this patient to being unable to endure living with it?**

The February email supplies direct notice.

The March GP referral escalates it.

The nursing message corroborates severity.

The later 2023 clinic note makes the explanatory conflict explicit.

This creates a second choice-space:

- clarify the meaning of the life-tolerability language;
- conduct or arrange risk assessment if clinically indicated;
- identify an urgent safeguarding owner;
- provide crisis/safety advice if clinically indicated;
- coordinate with the GP;
- intensify pain and movement-disorders review;
- reconsider the operative explanatory model;
- document why a safeguarding pathway was or was not considered necessary;
- ensure timed follow-up;
- preserve the safety signal in later care.

The current corpus shows real neurological and pain management activity.

It does **not yet show a documented safeguarding bridge** equivalent in visibility to the severity of the warnings.

That is a documentary negative and must remain one unless the complete record demonstrates otherwise.

The right formulation is:

> **The record shows substantial symptom management. What has not yet been identified is the structured safeguarding response, if any, to the life-safety signal itself.**

# 10. Irish law: the spotlight, not the substitute for evidence

This file is not legal advice.

The legal framework must remain underneath the facts.

## 10.1 Clinical negligence — Dunne

The starting Irish test remains the principles in **Dunne v National Maternity Hospital [1989] I.R. 91**.

The practical questions are:

- what would an ordinarily careful practitioner of equal specialist/general status and skill have done?
- was the actual course within the range ordinary competent practice permitted?
- if an approved practice is relied upon, did it contain an inherent defect obvious on due consideration?
- what does the relevant expert evidence establish about the required standard?

The Courts Service has continued to describe Dunne as the starting point for clinical-negligence analysis, with expert evidence ordinarily required to establish the standard.

Therefore this project must not try to prove breach by rhetoric.

It must present the choices and ask the expert:

> **Was the course actually taken one ordinary competent care permitted on the information then available?**

## 10.2 Information, patient voice and transfer

HIQA’s 2012 National Standards for Safer Better Healthcare applied across HSE healthcare and emphasised:

- person-centred care;
- safety;
- governance and accountability;
- use of information.

The 2013 national discharge-summary standard emphasised that relevant health information should reach the GP to support safe transfer from acute hospital to community care.

These materials do not themselves prove civil negligence.

They inform the surrounding professional and organisational expectations.

The relevant question is:

> **If clinically material patient information existed at a decision or transfer point, what should reasonable care and information governance have done with it?**

## 10.3 HSE Neurology Model of Care

The HSE Neurology Model of Care (2016) describes neurological services through an integrated service approach spanning hospital, primary and continuing care.

That matters because the case should never be framed as:

> Neurology **or** Podiatry.

The better question is:

> **How were Neurology, primary care and Podiatry supposed to work together where a neurological motor disorder, painful structural feet and gait/load problems overlapped?**

The existence of a Podiatry destination is not proof that the causal relationship had been answered.

## 10.4 Complaints/governance law

Part 9 of the Health Act 2004 is relevant to the administrative/governance layer.

Section 46 recognises complaints about actions said not to accord with fair and sound administrative practice, including actions alleged to result from negligence/carelessness or to be based on erroneous or incomplete information.

Section 48 excludes matters relating **solely** to the exercise of clinical judgment from that complaints route.

This distinction helps keep the project clean:

- **clinical negligence:** what reasonable care required;
- **documentation/handover:** what clinically material information should have travelled;
- **governance:** what information later reviewers used and whether the founding event was reconstructed completely.

The governance framework is not a substitute for the Dunne clinical test.

# 11. The information-state argument

The case is not only about what happened to the body.

It is also about what happened to the information.

The core distinction is:

> **state of investigation** versus **state of medical knowledge**.

A specialist letter saying:

> **“Not clear”**

can be read by a GP as:

> **the specialist investigated the problem and the remaining uncertainty is genuine clinical uncertainty.**

But if the relevant relationship was not adequately investigated, then:

> **investigative incompleteness may have been transmitted as specialist uncertainty.**

That is a critical information-flow proposition.

It is expert-dependent.

The 2017 Protest, if established, adds another layer.

Without it, the specialist account reads as uncertainty.

With it, the same account would have existed beside evidence that:

> **the patient contemporaneously disputed whether the painful problem had been dealt with at all.**

Those are different information states.

The omission of the Protest would therefore matter because:

> **it may cause a contested closure to travel as apparently uncontested specialist uncertainty.**

> **“before we get into trouble here.”**

This is a major escalation in evidential status.

The question becomes:

> **What safeguarding and clinical response would an ordinary competent specialist service be expected to make to an urgent GP referral framed in those terms?**

# 17. The “facade” problem

The GP’s use of “facade” creates a recurrent evidential theme.

The patient has repeatedly been described as:

- intelligent;
- articulate;
- insightful;
- resilient;
- capable of functioning through severe symptoms.

Those strengths may also obscure severity.

The case should not romanticise resilience.

It should ask:

> **Was the patient repeatedly at risk of being mistaken for how well he could carry what was happening to him?**

In 2017, adaptation and preserved cognition could coexist with profound motor abnormality.

In 2023, coherent speech and positive statements about mood could coexist with pain that the GP had described as producing a death wish.

This is not proof of hidden suicidality.

It is a caution against using composure as a substitute for direct assessment of risk.

# 18. The record-absorption test

Every new medical record should be processed through this document before it is allowed to change the case.

Ask:

## A. Does it change the founding presentation?

Does it show that feet/gait/tone/pain were more or less integrated than currently understood?

## B. Does it change the founding examination?

Does it show a missing relationship-directed foot/neurological examination actually occurred?

## C. Does it earn the separation?

Does it supply positive evidence that the painful feet were neurologically independent?

## D. Does it weaken separation?

Does it show medication, OFF state, dystonia, gait, loading or posture affecting the feet?

## E. Does it show notice?

Who knew what and when?

## F. Does it show response after notice?

What did the clinician/service actually do?

## G. Does it show ownership?

Who was responsible for the unresolved relationship or safety issue after the encounter?

## H. Does it change the safety chronology?

Does it contain life-tolerability, suicidality, crisis, risk assessment, safety planning or mental-health intervention?

## I. Does it change causation?

Would the new fact make earlier effective treatment more or less probable?

## J. Is it merely a new label?

A new label does not automatically change the case.

If it does not answer the relationship, ownership, notice, response or causation questions, it remains local information.

# 21. Defence stress test

A good coordinating document must preserve the strongest defence.

## Defence 1 — real structural pathology

True.

The feet had genuine structural abnormalities.

Podiatry was clinically appropriate.

Response:

> **Structural pathology does not establish structural independence from abnormal neurological posture, gait, loading or tone.**

## Defence 2 — patient distinguished foot pain from bodily tension

True.

The specialist account records that the patient “markedly divorced” them.

Response:

> **Phenomenological difference does not prove mechanistic independence. A patient can distinguish two sensations while still having one condition contribute to both.**

## Defence 3 — Neurology did substantial work

True.

This was not a cursory admission.

Response:

> **The case is not that nobody looked. It is that sophistication in disease classification does not by itself demonstrate that the source-of-pain relationship was investigated.**

## Defence 4 — Podiatry was already involved

True.

Response:

> **Treatment of painful structures is not the same as determining why those structures are painful in a patient with a severe neurological gait disorder.**

## Defence 5 — later Neurology did reconsider dystonia/neuropathy/OFF phenomena

True.

Response:

> **That weakens any claim of eight years of total non-reassessment. It strengthens the narrower question of when the accumulated crossing evidence required reconstruction of the founding relationship.**

## Defence 6 — the patient sometimes said the pain was not Parkinson-related

True.

Response:

> **The record is mixed. That is why the duty is to investigate the relationship rather than assume either complete neurological unity or complete separation.**

## Defence 7 — the 2023 patient also described mood as positive

True.

Response:

> **That makes direct risk assessment more, not less, important if the same period also contains “death wish” and “no quality of life.” It does not permit retrospective diagnosis of suicidality, but it prevents simple inference from apparent mood.**

## Defence 8 — pain management activity occurred after the safety warnings

True.

Response:

> **The safeguarding question is not whether pain treatment occurred. It is whether the life-safety signal itself received an appropriate documented response and ownership.**

# 24. Governance: the later institution must not review only the path that survived

The governance problem should be framed as a methodology question.

A later reviewer can be misled by longitudinal continuity.

If the founding record converted unresolved pain into “possibly separate / Not clear,” later notes may naturally inherit those categories.

Then a retrospective review may see:

> years of records using those categories

and treat the continuity as evidence that the categories were sound.

But continuity can arise from inheritance.

Therefore later governance should ask:

1. What was the original integrated problem?
2. What examination earned separation?
3. Was the Protest raised?
4. What patient voice travelled?
5. What patient voice did not?
6. What later medication evidence crossed the separation?
7. What did the 2019 GP reintegration mean?
8. What did the 2023 direct safety notice mean?
9. What did the GP “death wish” warning mean?
10. What did the explicit neurological misalignment mean?
11. Was there a documented safeguarding response?
12. Did the reviewer reconstruct the original choice-space, or only review the path that became history?

The governing warning is:

> **A later record can validate an earlier category merely by repeatedly inheriting it. That is not the same thing as independently proving the category was well founded.**
